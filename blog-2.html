The Art of Handmade Pasta: Why We Refuse to Cut Corners

June 28, 2026 · 5 min read · By Chef Marco Rossi

Every morning at 6:30am, before the first delivery arrives and long before the dining room fills, our pasta chef arrives at Ember & Thyme with flour-dusted hands and a sense of quiet purpose. What happens in the next three hours determines whether tonight's handmade pasta will be extraordinary — or merely good. We only accept one of those outcomes.

Why Handmade Still Matters in 2026

In an era of high-efficiency kitchen equipment and perfectly consistent extruded pasta, you might wonder why any restaurant would choose to spend three hours doing by hand what a machine can do in minutes. The answer is texture — and texture is everything.

When pasta dough is worked by hand, something happens that no machine can replicate. The gluten develops differently. The surface becomes slightly irregular — not from sloppiness, but from the natural variation of human touch. Those micro-imperfections are what sauce clings to. They're why our Handmade Pasta with brown butter and sage tastes the way it does.

"A machine makes pasta. Hands make memories. The difference is in every single bite."

The Process: From Flour to Fork

We use only two ingredients in our base pasta dough: double-zero flour milled in Italy and eggs from a heritage breed farm in Prince Edward County. No additives. No stabilizers. No shortcuts.

Step 1 — The Dough

The flour is measured by weight, not volume. The eggs are cracked one by one, their yolks checked for colour — deep orange means the hens are healthy and well-fed, which means the pasta will have that beautiful golden hue our guests have come to expect. The dough is mixed by hand until it just comes together, then rested for exactly 30 minutes.

Step 2 — The Rest

This step is non-negotiable. Rushing the rest produces tough, elastic dough that fights back when you roll it. Properly rested dough is pliable, silky, and almost cooperative — it wants to be thin.

Step 3 — The Roll

We use a traditional wooden rolling pin, not a pasta machine. The dough is rolled in stages, rotating 90 degrees between passes until it reaches the thickness of a playing card. Then — and only then — it's cut.

What We're Really Talking About When We Talk About Quality

Our handmade pasta costs more than dried pasta. It takes longer to make. It requires skilled hands and real attention. We charge accordingly — and our guests understand why, because they can taste the difference the moment their fork touches it.

This is what we mean when we say Ember & Thyme refuses to cut corners. Not as a slogan. As a daily commitment that starts before the sun comes up and ends when the last plate returns to the kitchen clean.

Come try it. Order the pasta. Close your eyes for the first bite. That's three hours of morning work, two ingredients, and decades of tradition — arriving at your table in a single, perfect forkful.
